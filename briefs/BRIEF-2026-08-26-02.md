## Tennis — your free-roll idea, and a "contradiction" that was never there

**As of 2026-08-26.** Written by the `tennis` session.

**Your Principal Recovery idea: twelve of the thirteen ways I tried it fail, and
one works.**

**First, what it costs.** Taking a dollar off the table is not free — you pay a
fee the person who just holds never pays. **At 20 cents it costs about 8 cents
for every dollar you take back.** And here is the part that works against your
own example: **the cheaper the contract, the worse that fee is as a share of the
sale.** At 10 cents it is 11%; at 90 cents it is 1%. Your 10c-to-20c example is
close to the most expensive place on the board to do this.

**Second, the thing that surprised me and that I had predicted wrong.** I
expected this to lose a little money and genuinely calm things down. **It does
lose money — and it usually makes the bad stretches WORSE, by up to 87%.** The
reason is simple once you see it: selling half your winners clips the recoveries
that would have climbed you out of a losing run, while the losers still lose in
full. **You keep the losses and cut short the repairs.**

**Third — one version does work, and it is the one you would probably have
picked.** Selling when the price is **1.5 times what you paid**: slightly more
money than just holding, and the worst losing stretch drops by 9% across
everything, and by 88% on the bets where it actually fires. **That is exactly
the trade you described wanting.** I checked it against a fake version that sells
at a random moment, and the fake version is 34 to 39 points worse — so the
timing is doing real work.

**I am not calling it a win yet.** It is one survivor out of thirteen things I
tried, and that is exactly what a lucky fluke looks like. It needs testing on
matches nobody has looked at.

**One warning about your worked example.** A contract cannot double from above
50 cents — there is a ceiling at a dollar. Our main tennis bet buys at about 70
cents, so **679 of its 738 bets could never have doubled.** Not "did not" —
could not. Any doubling rule is unavailable to most of what we trade.

**The one way this could genuinely make money, I could not test.** If cash is
tight, getting your stake back early lets you place the next bet sooner. To
measure that you need a strategy that actually makes money and is short of cash.
**Tennis has none. Baseball does** — the tool is built so it can be pointed
there unchanged.

**Separately: a disagreement between two tennis populations turned out not to
exist.** The coordinating chat found an effect on the small tournaments but not
the main tour and could not explain it. **The main tour result was measured on
56 to 136 matches — it cannot tell a 1-point effect from a 6-point one.** There
was nothing to explain; one side was simply too small to see anything.

**Both open questions are settled by the same wait: about three weeks, then
re-download and re-run.** Costs nothing and needs no new machinery.

**Nothing needed from you.**

**Your take-profit and stop-loss idea: tested at 81 different settings, and the
answer is that stopping out is what loses the money.**

Holding until the game ends made **$31.33**. **Every single one of the 72
settings that used a stop-loss lost money.** A stop-loss on its own, with no
take-profit, was the worst thing on the board — it lost between **$192 and
$496**.

**One setting did beat holding** — take profit at 25 cents, no stop, $41.00.
**I do not believe it and neither should you**, for three reasons I wrote down
before running it: it is the best of 81 tries; the setting one step away makes
only **$7.18**, so a 5-cent change costs $34, which is not how a real effect
behaves; and **selling at a random moment produces anything from −$95 to +$47**,
which contains it.

**The reason stops fail is simple once you see the prices.** A baseball price
swings wildly once the game starts. So a stop gets hit on nearly every bet at
some point, turning a bet that would have been fine into a locked-in loss.

**Your cash-out question: you were right.** You spotted $5.87 to sell against
$6.00 held. Across 6,115 minutes where a bet was at 90 cents or better,
**cashing out early costs about 1.2% of the payout**, and up to about 4%. Your
"1 to 2%" was correct.

**One of the sixteen bots has never placed a single bet in 13 days, and I now
know why.** It watches for missing players. **It found 474 games with a real
absence** — so it is not that they are rare. The problem is that what it thinks
a missing player is worth (about one and a half cents) is **smaller than the
cost of placing the bet** (about three and a half cents). Its best opportunity
in 13 days was still a loser.

**That is the same story for three of the five ideas**, and the ordering is
exact: the only one making money is the only one whose signal is bigger than the
cost of trading it.

**I have deliberately NOT fixed it by changing the number**, because the number
is a guess nobody has ever measured, and turning it up until the bot bets would
be choosing the dial to get the answer. **What I want to do instead is measure
what the real price does when a player is scratched** — that needs no guess at
all, and I now have the prices to do it.
