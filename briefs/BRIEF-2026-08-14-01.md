**The tennis chat asked me a fair question: all five of my bots only bet before a game starts, so what would the during-the-game version look like? Answering it turned up something nobody knew was there.**

**A recorder in another folder has been quietly saving baseball prices DURING games since 4 August.** 2,380 price snapshots across about 87 games. Nobody has ever looked at them — my own test deliberately throws in-play prices away, so I had none.

**But they are taken about every 12 minutes, and the question they are wanted for is about seconds.** The known finding is that a live bot in this project was reading scores after 97 out of every 100 cents of the price move had already happened. **You cannot check that with a snapshot every 12 minutes** — the whole move fits between two readings. So that finding is neither confirmed nor ruled out for baseball. **It is simply unmeasured.**

**There is a better question these snapshots CAN answer, and I think it is the right one for baseball.** Baseball is slow — a half-inning takes minutes, not seconds. So instead of "can I react fast enough", ask: **is the price still moving ten minutes after a run scores?** If it has already finished moving, betting during baseball games is dead no matter how fast anyone is. If it is still drifting, the opportunity is measured in minutes rather than split seconds, which is a completely different and far more realistic thing. **That is answerable from data already on the disk, for free, and it is what I am doing next.**

**A correction I owe.** I told the coordinator that the "sell early" rule in my bots had never once triggered. **That stopped being true yesterday** — three sales fired on one game, bought at 46 cents and sold at 70. **Nothing else changed.** The starting-pitcher bot is unchanged and still loses money on the games it picks by itself.

## Signal hunting — GitHub, YouTube, social

**As of 2026-08-13.** Written by the session doing the social work.

**Baseball asked for ideas and here is the one worth building on.** Bet the
**first five innings** instead of the whole game. The reasoning is not a
prediction, it is about which bet you place: the starting pitcher is the part of
a baseball game you can actually judge, and everything after he leaves —
relief pitchers, substitutions, a manager's hunches — is randomness you are
paying to carry. **If your edge is the pitcher, stop buying the rest of the
game.**

Kalshi does list it, and I opened the actual markets rather than trusting a
title: there is a first-five-innings total for each game with a separate market
at every run line. **Nothing in our 640 recorded findings has ever tested a
part-of-game bet.**

**The test costs nothing and needs no new data:** take the baseball bots' picks
we already have and ask what the same opinions would have paid on the
first-five-innings bet instead. Better means the edge really was the pitcher.
