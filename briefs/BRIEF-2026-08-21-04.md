## Tennis — the patient-buyer test is finished, and the answer is "we can't tell, and we won't be able to"

**As of 2026-08-21.** Written by the `tennis` session.

<!-- COORDINATOR-STATE

left: nothing on this thread - the answer is that the data cannot settle it and the shortfall is nine-fold

**The question was yours: the tennis "buy the fallen favourite" bet loses money
mostly because you pay to jump the queue. What if you waited in the queue
instead?**

**Waiting works. There is just nothing underneath it.** On 738 matches between
14 June and 1 August:

- paying to jump the queue lost about **$3.40 for every $100 risked**
- waiting your turn made about **$1.20** — if you were first in line
- and lost about **$4.00** if you were last in line

**We cannot tell which of those you'd be**, because the exchange doesn't publish
how many people are already queued ahead of you. And the good end of that range
is well inside what pure luck produces anyway.

**Three things we did learn, and one of them I got wrong.**

**I predicted we'd struggle to get served. Wrong — we got served on 95 matches
out of 100.** That was my main worry and it isn't one.

**Which queue you join matters a lot.** Every match has two prices that add up
to a dollar. Backing the underdog directly gets you served 90 times in 100;
doing the identical bet from the other side — offering to sell the favourite —
gets you served 95 times in 100, and 80 rather than 53 when the queue is long.
Same bet, much better line. Worth remembering for anything else we try.

**Your bigger-drop idea was right about the direction, for the third time.** The
deeper the fall we wait for, the better it gets: from losing $8 per $100 at a
small drop to losing $2 at a 40-cent drop. **But most of that isn't a better
bet — it's a smaller toll.** Kalshi's fee is biggest at 50 cents and shrinks
towards the edges, so buying at 78 cents is simply cheaper to do. The actual
mispricing stays flat and never once separates from zero.

**The part worth your attention.** To prove this either way you'd need about
**8,000 matches**. Kalshi only lets us reach back about ten weeks, and
everything reachable adds up to **902**. Collecting forward happens at about
**9 matches a day** — so roughly **two and a half years** to get an answer.

**I am not proposing we wait.** The best guess is already sitting on zero, so
two and a half years would most likely buy us a confident "no". **I'd rather
spend the attention on something with a bigger claimed edge.**

**Nothing needed from you.**

## Baseball — paper forward test

**As of 2026-08-20.** Written by the `mlb-paper` session.

<!-- COORDINATOR-STATE

**Your take-profit and stop-loss idea: tested at 81 different settings, and the
answer is that stopping out is what loses the money.**

Holding until the game ends made **$31.33**. **Every single one of the 72
settings that used a stop-loss lost money.** A stop-loss on its own, with no
take-profit, was the worst thing on the board — it lost between **$192 and
$496**.

**One setting did beat holding** — take profit at 25 cents, no stop, $41.00.
**I do not believe it and neither should you**, for three reasons I wrote down
before running it: it is the best of 81 tries; the setting one step away makes
only **$7.18**, so a 5-cent change costs $34, which is not how a real effect
behaves; and **selling at a random moment produces anything from −$95 to +$47**,
which contains it.

**The reason stops fail is simple once you see the prices.** A baseball price
swings wildly once the game starts. So a stop gets hit on nearly every bet at
some point, turning a bet that would have been fine into a locked-in loss.

**Your cash-out question: you were right.** You spotted $5.87 to sell against
$6.00 held. Across 6,115 minutes where a bet was at 90 cents or better,
**cashing out early costs about 1.2% of the payout**, and up to about 4%. Your
"1 to 2%" was correct.

**One of the sixteen bots has never placed a single bet in 13 days, and I now
know why.** It watches for missing players. **It found 474 games with a real
absence** — so it is not that they are rare. The problem is that what it thinks
a missing player is worth (about one and a half cents) is **smaller than the
cost of placing the bet** (about three and a half cents). Its best opportunity
in 13 days was still a loser.

**That is the same story for three of the five ideas**, and the ordering is
exact: the only one making money is the only one whose signal is bigger than the
cost of trading it.

**I have deliberately NOT fixed it by changing the number**, because the number
is a guess nobody has ever measured, and turning it up until the bot bets would
be choosing the dial to get the answer. **What I want to do instead is measure
what the real price does when a player is scratched** — that needs no guess at
all, and I now have the prices to do it.

**The real blocker is now clear.** Most strategies we have written need to know
things about the *world* — when goals were scored, which clubs played, when
teams last played. We save prices beautifully and none of that. That is the
next problem.
