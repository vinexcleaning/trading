**Your take-profit and stop-loss idea: tested at 81 different settings, and the
answer is that stopping out is what loses the money.**

Holding until the game ends made **$31.33**. **Every single one of the 72
settings that used a stop-loss lost money.** A stop-loss on its own, with no
take-profit, was the worst thing on the board — it lost between **$192 and
$496**.

**One setting did beat holding** — take profit at 25 cents, no stop, $41.00.
**I do not believe it and neither should you**, for three reasons I wrote down
before running it: it is the best of 81 tries; the setting one step away makes
only **$7.18**, so a 5-cent change costs $34, which is not how a real effect
behaves; and **selling at a random moment produces anything from −$95 to +$47**,
which contains it.

**The reason stops fail is simple once you see the prices.** A baseball price
swings wildly once the game starts. So a stop gets hit on nearly every bet at
some point, turning a bet that would have been fine into a locked-in loss.

**Your cash-out question: you were right.** You spotted $5.87 to sell against
$6.00 held. Across 6,115 minutes where a bet was at 90 cents or better,
**cashing out early costs about 1.2% of the payout**, and up to about 4%. Your
"1 to 2%" was correct.

**One of the sixteen bots has never placed a single bet in 13 days, and I now
know why.** It watches for missing players. **It found 474 games with a real
absence** — so it is not that they are rare. The problem is that what it thinks
a missing player is worth (about one and a half cents) is **smaller than the
cost of placing the bet** (about three and a half cents). Its best opportunity
in 13 days was still a loser.

**That is the same story for three of the five ideas**, and the ordering is
exact: the only one making money is the only one whose signal is bigger than the
cost of trading it.

be choosing the dial to get the answer. **What I want to do instead is measure

## De-vig, totals, and the props door

**As of 2026-08-20.** All twenty-one coordinator messages answered.

**Totals are dead — three families, in one hour, before any game finished.** Run totals, first-five-innings runs and team totals. **109 prices across nine games, and not one is worth trading in either direction.** This was the biggest untouched corner on the board: run totals alone are 2,280 markets on the recorder and nobody had ever written a strategy against them.

**⚠ And I nearly told you the opposite.** One team-total showed a gap of **2.79 cents against a 1.68 cent cost** — bigger than the cost, which looks like a trade. The check said no, so I looked at why instead of believing the flag:

> Kalshi will sell you the over at 36 cents and buy it back at 32. The honest price is 33.2. **So buying costs you 2.8 cents too much — and betting the other way costs you 1.2 cents too much, at the same time.** Both directions overpriced at once. That is not a contradiction; that is what the gap between the buy and sell price *is*.

**That turned out to explain every dead result here.** Across all 109 prices, **the honest price sits inside Kalshi's own buy-sell gap 70 times out of 100** — and the wider that gap, the more often it happens (57 times in 100 at a one-cent gap, 78 at three). **So the market that looks like it disagrees most is just the one with the widest spread.** There is no disagreement to trade; there is a spread to pay. It also tells us in advance where the next test of this kind will fail.

**One thing worth knowing before anyone builds on this.** The honest bookmaker only quotes the three or four prices nearest the likely score. Kalshi quotes the whole range. **So the cheap end — where trading costs a fifth of a cent instead of nearly two — is exactly the part nobody can check.** 179 prices sit there. That is **not** a reason to think they are wrong; it is that we would have no cheap way of finding out if we were.

**As of 2026-08-20.**

Its job is to invent Kalshi strategies in bulk, kill most of them cheaply, and
paper-trade the few that survive. Nothing here can spend money.

**The tester is built and it has run. The answer is no, so far.** Buying at the

should be. That is the expected answer, and it is the one we said in advance we
expected: the plain, all-markets version of anything is normally flat or

**Only one group of markets has enough finished events to say anything at all**
— sports, 528 of them, losing about 3 cents a contract. Everything else is two
days of saved prices and the report says so on every line.

**The most useful thing the tester did was catch its own fault.** It has a fake
control built in, which should shuffle the results and prove the machine can
tell a real pattern from noise. It returned the identical number twenty times
running. It turned out the shuffle could not possibly change the answer — a
control that cannot move is decoration, and it would have waved through every
future test. Replaced with one that works.

**Two things found that matter more than the money.** Most markets have **no
price on both sides an hour before they finish**, so a lot of what looks
tradeable cannot actually be traded. And the financial markets **only absorb
about 38 dollars** before the offers run out — asking for 50, 200 or 500 all
get you about the same 38. Something that only works in the first 38 dollars is
a hobby, and we know that now instead of after a month.

**And I found a fault in my own data collection, late.** My settlement
download stopped at 8,000 records per market type -- my own limit, not the
exchange's. Six of the busiest markets were cut in half without saying so. The
full set is nearly double what I first tested on. The conclusion did not change,
but it had been resting on half the data and nothing would have told me. Fixed,
and it now shouts when it hits a limit instead of quietly reporting the ceiling
as the answer.

**The real blocker is now clear.** Most strategies we have written need to know
things about the *world* — when goals were scored, which clubs played, when
teams last played. We save prices beautifully and none of that. That is the
next problem.
